> **TL;DR:** Every empty moment in my day got filled with fast dopamine, and I never decided that. When I uninstalled the apps, my fingers kept going to where they used to be. Slow things got my brain back to a slower pace, and a week later I spent 30 minutes doing absolutely nothing. It was the best rest I'd had in a long time.

We forgot how to be bored. Every gap we used to have between one thing and another got filled, with doom scrolling, short videos, instant answers, fast dopamine, and nobody sat down and decided that. It just happened. And I think all this trash is making ourselves dumb, useless and dopamine addicted. Me included.

A few weeks ago I read an article by James Anderson, [I think we're forgetting how to be bored](https://dev.to/james_anderson_h/i-think-were-forgetting-how-to-be-bored-3pe5). His point is that boredom was never empty time. It's the place where your mind wanders and connects ideas, and we filled it so slowly that reaching for the phone became automatic before anyone noticed.

I agreed with him. But agreeing with an article is easy. What I want to tell you is what happened when I actually took the apps off my phone and lived with the gap for ten days.

## Every gap got filled, and nobody decided it

I can tell you thousands of times this happened to me. On the bus, going somewhere. Waiting for someone. Waiting a few minutes before a meeting. Any gap, any one, and the phone was already in my hand.

The worst ones were the anxious moments. When I was anxious about something, about my future, I always caught myself running to social media, short videos, anything that could bring me fast dopamine. Anything to avoid the loneliness, or the bore times, or whatever. I didn't want to be alone with that feeling, so I gave my brain something louder to look at.

Anderson describes this well. Nobody chose to kill idle time. The kettle, the elevator, the page loading, all those little dead moments got filled one by one, until the reach for the phone wasn't a decision anymore. It was just what your hand does.

## My fingers still knew where the apps were

So I uninstalled social media. I even removed YouTube from my phone, because I knew the short videos would get me there too.

And then the funny thing happened. I caught myself taking the phone almost without thinking, and my fingers going directly to where those apps used to be located. Nothing there. Just an empty spot on the screen where the dopamine used to live.

My first reaction was like, whoah, what am I doing here?

I wasn't opening the apps because I wanted something. I wasn't even thinking. My thumb knew the way by itself. The habit lived in my fingers.

## This wasn't my first try

This is not the first time I uninstalled these apps. I tried it some other times before, and it didn't stick.

The FOMO appears. Of course it does. I still think that I'm losing something, that something is happening out there and I'm not seeing it. My brain wants that fast dopamine, and it lets me know.

But this time is different. Don't know exactly why yet. Let's see.

## Slow dopamine is the ramp, not the destination

When I realized my thumb was hitting an empty spot, I didn't go straight to doing nothing. I don't think I could. I really missed those things, so I went straight to good things to do in those moments. Real dopamine.

I went for a walk. I took a book and started reading. I opened my 3DS emulator and started playing Pokémon. Very slow games, very slow things, just to put my head in the place. And man, with that I felt so much better.

Is that still filling the gap? Yes, it is. I won't pretend a walk or a Pokémon battle is the same as being bored. But doing these slow things is what made me able to do nothing later. It's like it, don't know, reconfigured my brain? My brain still wants the fast dopamine. I just give real dopamine to him.

Another thing that is helping me a lot is an app called [I Am Sober](https://iamsober.com). It's made for a lot of addictions, and it has a community, with a specific space for social media and short videos addiction. There you find other people that are going through the same thing, people that are with you.

I use it to track the days, and I post there daily. And yes, it stopped me. The app has a function for urges, with some exercises you can do when the feeling hits, to get through it and forget it. When the pull comes, I have something to do with my hands that isn't opening an app.

## Thirty minutes of nothing

Then something happened that changed everything.

It was about a week after I uninstalled the apps. I work from home, and it was right before my lunch time finished. I was just there. I had nothing to do, literally nothing. Nothing in a rush, nothing with a deadline. I didn't want to watch any series, I didn't want to play anything, I didn't want to go anywhere.

So I went to my bed and stayed there. Doing nothing, in silence.

That was nothing sad. That was nothing heavy. That felt great.

I was relaxing. I was resting, really resting, for the first time in a long time. My brain was resting, and I wasn't upset about doing nothing. I didn't feel guilty or lazy or behind. I just rested.

It lasted like 30 minutes. And it was the best 30 minutes of rest that I had in a long time.

Think about how strange that is. I spent so many hours lying down with the phone in my face, calling it rest. And the best rest I had in a long time was the one where I did absolutely nothing.

## What showed up when I was simply there

Anderson says some ideas only show up when you're idle, in the shower, on a walk, right before you sleep. I can confirm that, and it was more than ideas.

In these ten days a lot of thoughts showed up in those quiet gaps. I had much less brain fog. Much less standard thinking, the automatic stuff you think because you just saw someone else think it. I started to do things without being in a rush.

And the quality of the things I do in my day to day increased a lot.

A simple example is setting up my daily overview. Preparing the next day, thinking about the actual day I'm living. Before, that was something I'd rush through between one scroll and another. Now I actually sit with it and think, and the plan comes out better because I was there when I made it.

> The quality of what I do went up because I was simply there.

Nothing magic. I just stopped leaving my own life every time there was a gap in it.

## "But scrolling is how I rest"

I know what some people will say. Scrolling is how they rest. They had a long day, they deserve some videos, boredom is just wasted time.

I don't think they're partly right here. Not even a little.

Remember why I opened those apps in the first place. A lot of times it was anxiety about my future. I wanted to escape that feeling. And did the scrolling help? No. The anxiety still came back, every single time.

Worse, it came back bigger. Because a lot of that anxiety is related to comparison, and every short video came with a comparison. Someone richer. Someone that succeeded more. Someone more intelligent. Etc, etc. The feed never runs out of people doing better than you.

So I was running from anxiety about my future straight into a machine that shows me, one video after another, everyone who is ahead of me. That's not rest. The escape was feeding the exact thing I was escaping from.

Now compare that with the 30 minutes on my bed. No feed, nobody to compare myself with. Just silence.

That's why I call it trash. Social media and short videos, the way they are built, are making ourselves dumb, useless and dopamine addicted. I say ourselves because I'm in that list. I was the one with the thumb going to the empty spot.

---

I'm not going to tell you to uninstall anything. I'm ten days in, and I already failed at this before, so I'm not the guy to give you a plan.

I can only tell you what I found when the noise stopped. My bed and the silence, nothing to do and no rush to do it. Nothing sad. Nothing heavy.

Just my brain, finally resting, after a long time.
